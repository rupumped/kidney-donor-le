\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Model overview}
I constructed a Markov state-transition model to compare lifetime outcomes for two hypothetical cohorts of otherwise identical individuals: one cohort that donates a kidney and one that does not. Both cohorts enter the model at the age of donation in full health, and the simulation advances in annual cycles until age~110. The analysis was conducted from the perspective of the individual donor rather than the health-care system; outcomes are reported as undiscounted remaining life-years, consistent with the convention in burden-of-disease analyses.~\cite{murray_gbd_2012} The base-case age at donation is 40 years; subgroup analyses span ages 25--55. I used a Monte Carlo simulation with $5 \times 10^6$ individuals per arm and cross-validated against a deterministic analytic cohort implementation. I used annual (one-year) cycles throughout.
% Life-years accumulated with an end-of-cycle convention; the resulting systematic underestimate of approximately 0.5~years applies equally to both arms and cancels in $\Delta$LE.
The Markov model structure is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:markov}.

\subsection{Health states}
The model contains seven mutually exclusive health states (Figure~\ref{fig:markov}): \textit{Healthy}, \textit{ESRD onset}, \textit{Dialysis}, \textit{Standard waitlist}, \textit{Priority waitlist}, \textit{Post-transplant}, and \textit{Dead} (absorbing). At ESRD onset, the cohort branches: a fraction is listed preemptively (bypassing dialysis entirely) and the remainder initiates hemodialysis. The donor arm routes ESRD survivors to the \textit{Priority waitlist}, reflecting the allocation priority afforded to prior living donors (PLD) under KAS250; the non-donor arm routes to the \textit{Standard waitlist}. Graft failure returns patients to the \textit{Dialysis} state, bypassing the ESRD-onset branch.

\subsection{ESRD risk after donation}
Fifteen-year cumulative ESRD incidence was taken from Muzaale et al.~\cite{muzaale_risk_2014}: 30.8 per 10,000 (95\%~CI 24.3--38.5) for donors and 3.9 per 10,000 (95\%~CI 0.8--8.9) for controls matched by age, sex, and health (8-fold relative risk). Race-specific donor rates were 74.7/10,000 (Black) and 22.7/10,000 (White).~\cite{muzaale_risk_2014} The White non-donor baseline was taken from Grams et al.~\cite{grams_kidney-failure_2016} (sex-averaged 0.05\%/15~yr), because zero ESRD events were recorded in White matched controls in Muzaale et al.

I modeled ESRD hazard with a Weibull distribution. I fit the shape parameter ($k = 2.54$) from the five cumulative-incidence-of-ESRD curves (median, interquartile range, and 1st/99th percentiles) that Massie et al.\ report at 5, 10, 15, and 20 years post-donation.~\cite{massie_quantifying_2017} The Weibull CDF linearizes under the complementary log-log transform, $\ln(-\ln(1-I(t))) = k\ln(t) - k\ln(\lambda)$, so ordinary least-squares regression of the transformed incidence on $\ln(t)$ recovers $k$ directly as the slope; applied to the median curve this gives $k=2.54$, reproducing all four published points to within 5\%. Fitting the same regression separately to each of the other four published curves gives $k$ ranging 2.15--2.54, confirming a single accelerating Weibull shape ($k>1$) is broadly consistent across the reported risk distribution; a constant-hazard ($k=1$) model is ruled out, as it would predict only $\approx$21/10,000 at 20 years from the observed 15-year rate of 16/10,000, versus the 34/10,000 actually reported. Once fit, $k$ was combined with a per-subgroup Weibull scale $\lambda$, calibrated via a competing-risk bisection algorithm to reproduce each subgroup's 15-year cumulative incidence while accounting for background-mortality attrition in the at-risk pool. I drew within-donor subgroup hazard ratios from Massie et al.: 2.96 (95\%~CI 2.25--3.89) for Black race, 1.88 (95\%~CI 1.50--2.35) for male sex, and 1.40 per decade (non-Black donors).~\cite{massie_quantifying_2017}

\subsection{Dialysis survival and waitlist listing}
First-year hemodialysis mortality was 22\% and subsequent-year mortality 17\% ($\approx$42\% five-year survival), consistent with USRDS estimates.~\cite{usrds_2024}

At ESRD onset, a fraction of patients is listed preemptively before dialysis initiation, avoiding dialysis mortality entirely. I drew preemptive listing probabilities from USRDS 2025 Annual Data Report (ADR) Figure~7.13: 5.8\% overall for the non-donor arm and 9.4\% for the donor arm, using the age 18--44 population rate as a proxy for this younger, healthier, donor-like cohort (USRDS does not report preemptive-listing rates stratified by donor status).~\cite{usrds_2025}

Among patients on dialysis, the conditional annual probability of joining the waitlist was set to 15\%/year (base case), derived from USRDS 2025 ADR
Figure~7.17.~\cite{usrds_2025} Approximately 25\% of 18--44 year-old ESRD patients are listed or transplanted within one year, of whom $\approx$9.4\% were preemptively listed, yielding an estimated post-dialysis listing rate of $\approx$15.6\%/year for the donor-like cohort.

\subsection{Transplant waiting time and waitlist outcomes}
Transplant probability while listed was modeled as a time-homogeneous exponential process with rate $r = 1/\mu$, where $\mu$ is the mean waiting time, the natural parameterization of the exponential distribution.~\cite{sonnenberg1993} For the standard waitlist, I used the post-KAS250 national mean of 1{,}765~days.~\cite{punjala_determinants_2024} For the PLD priority waitlist, I used the post-KAS median of 102.6~days,~\cite{wainright_impact_2017} converted to a mean via $\mu = \text{median}/\ln 2 \approx 148$~days. Waitlist mortality was 5.0 deaths per 100 patient-years (SRTR 2023 ADR Figure~KI~24). Competing removal (delisting for reasons other than transplant or death) was derived from the 3-year cumulative removal incidence of 19.06\% reported in SRTR 2023 ADR Figure~KI~22, back-calculated to a cause-specific annual rate of 11.4\%/year via competing-risk bisection accounting for simultaneous attrition from transplantation and death.~\cite{srtr_2023}

\subsection{Post-transplant survival}
I derived post-transplant annual mortality from age-stratified 5-year Kaplan--Meier estimates for DDKT recipients in the SRTR 2023 ADR (Figure~KI~70, 2016--2018 cohort): 0.9\%/year (ages 18--34), 1.8\%/year (35--49), 3.9\%/year (50--64), and 6.9\%/year ($\geq 65$).~\cite{srtr_2023} The base case uses DDKT survival because prior living donors who develop ESRD predominantly receive transplants via the deceased-donor priority pathway rather than living-donor transplantation, consistent with USRDS data showing that living-donor transplants account for a small minority of transplants in this population.~\cite{muzaale2016outcomes} A one-way sensitivity analysis substitutes LDKT patient survival (SRTR 2023 ADR Figure~KI~76: 0.4\%, 0.8\%, 1.7\%, and 3.9\%/year by age band) to bound the effect of this assumption. Annual graft failure post-year~1 was likewise age-stratified, derived as $1-(S_5/S_1)^{1/4}$, where $S_n$ denotes the $n$-year graft survival probability, from age-specific 1- and 5-year DDKT graft survival in the SRTR 2023 ADR (Figure~KI~53, 2016--2018 cohort): 3.9\%/year (ages 18--34), 3.4\%/year (35--49), 4.9\%/year (50--64), and 7.8\%/year ($\geq 65$).~\cite{srtr_2023} Graft failure returned patients to the Dialysis state.

\subsection{Background mortality and donor all-cause survival}
I took background mortality in the Healthy state from the 2021 US Life Tables,~\cite{cdc_2023} with sex-specific tables used in sex-stratified analyses. As discussed in the Introduction, the published literature on donor all-cause mortality does not consist of noisy estimates of one fixed hazard ratio (HR): short-to-medium-follow-up cohorts and the meta-analysis pooling them report no excess mortality,~\cite{okeeffe_mid-_2018} while the one cohort followed long enough to plausibly detect a late-emerging effect reports a significantly elevated HR,~\cite{mjoeen_long-term_2014} with survival curves separating only after approximately ten years.~\cite{mjoeen_reply,okeeffe_reply_2018} I therefore modeled the donor all-cause mortality HR as time-varying rather than fixed: flat at 1.0 (no excess risk) for the first 10 years after donation, then increasing linearly to 1.30 by 15 years post-donation, matching the Mjøen et~al.~\cite{mjoeen_reply} cohort's median follow-up, and held flat at 1.30 thereafter. The non-donor arm's background mortality HR is fixed at 1.0 throughout. Because this reflects a genuine question about model structure (does excess mortality risk exist at all, and if so, when does it emerge) rather than sampling variation around an agreed-upon value, it is not included in the probabilistic sensitivity analysis; instead, I report a dedicated structural sensitivity analysis over the plausible alternative HR assumptions in the literature.

\subsection{Subgroup analyses}
I repeated the analyses at ages 25, 35, 40, 45, and 55~years at donation. Donor ESRD rates were scaled by the Massie~2017 age HR (1.40/decade, non-Black donors);~\cite{massie_quantifying_2017} I held non-donor baselines at the Muzaale overall matched-control rate.

I took race-specific donor ESRD rates (Black 74.7/10,000; White 22.7/10,000) and race-specific non-donor baselines (Black sex-averaged 0.195\%/15~yr; White sex-averaged 0.05\%/15~yr) from Muzaale et al.~\cite{muzaale_risk_2014} and Grams et al.,~\cite{grams_kidney-failure_2016} respectively. Race-specific waitlist mortality and post-transplant survival were from SRTR 2023 Figures~KI~25 and KI~71.~\cite{srtr_2023}

I derived sex-specific donor ESRD rates by applying the Massie~2017 male-sex HR (1.88)~\cite{massie_quantifying_2017} to the overall donor rate, weighted by the observed donor sex mix (63.5\% female; SRTR 2023 ADR Figure~KI~104).~\cite{srtr_2023} For non-donor baselines, I used the Grams~2016 White sex-specific rates (female 0.04\%/15~yr; male 0.06\%/15~yr)~\cite{grams_kidney-failure_2016} rather than applying the within-donor sex HR to the control arm, because Massie~2017 hazard ratios describe variation within the donor cohort and are not appropriate for scaling a different reference population. I applied this sourcing rule, Muzaale matched-control rates for overall analyses and Grams direct rates wherever sex or race is stratified, consistently across all simulations.

\subsection{Probabilistic sensitivity analysis (PSA)}
\label{sec:methods-psa}
I conducted a PSA with 2,000 parameter draws. Sampled quantities and their distributions were:
\begin{itemize}
	\item ESRD 15-year cumulative risks: beta distributions parameterized from the Muzaale~2014 published 95\%~CIs;\cite{muzaale_risk_2014}
	\item Weibull shape $k$: log-normal, $\sigma = 0.069$, the sample standard deviation of $\ln k$ across the five independent cloglog-regression fits described above (Massie et al.\ does not report a CI for this parameter directly); and
	\item Waitlist listing probability: beta, fractional SE~=~40\%, consistent with the 0.05--0.30 plausible range
\end{itemize}
Registry-derived parameters (from SRTR or USRDS) have negligible sampling error relative to structural uncertainty and were fixed at base-case values; scenario variation in these parameters is captured by the one-way sensitivity analysis. The donor all-cause mortality HR is deliberately excluded from the PSA: as described above, the disagreement among published estimates reflects a structural question about the time-course of donor risk, not sampling error around a single value, and is instead addressed by a separate, dedicated structural sensitivity analysis. I report the mean $\Delta\text{LE}$, 95\%~credible interval, and the probability that donation is beneficial to life-expectancy.

\subsection{One-way sensitivity analysis}
I varied each parameter individually, holding the donor all-cause mortality HR at its base-case time-varying profile:
\begin{itemize}
	\item No priority access and changing the PLD wait time
	\item ESRD relative risk from the Mjøen upper-bound on ESRD HR
	\item Post-transplant survival at LDKT quality
	\item Dialysis mortality~$\pm 50\%$
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Structural sensitivity to donor mortality hazard ratio}
\label{sec:methods-hr-structural}
Because the donor all-cause mortality HR is not sampled in the PSA or perturbed in the one-way sensitivity analysis above, I separately compared the base-case time-varying HR profile against the flat (time-invariant) HR implied by individual published estimates, taken at face value for the donor's entire remaining lifetime: 
% HR~$=0.60$ (the O'Keeffe et~al.\ pooled point estimate),~\cite{okeeffe_reply_2018} 
HR~$=1.0$ (no excess mortality), HR~$=1.30$ (the Mjøen et~al.\ point estimate),~\cite{mjoeen_long-term_2014} and HR~$=1.52$ (the upper bound of the Mjøen et~al.\ 95\%~CI). This analysis is intended to bound how much the result depends on which single-HR assumption is adopted, rather than to imply that any of these flat alternatives is itself a plausible model of donor risk.

\subsection{Voucher benefit}
\label{subsec:methods-voucher}
A kidney-donation ``voucher'' is a mechanism by which a living donor designates up to five healthy, non-donor family members to receive PLD priority-waitlist access should any one of those family members ever develop ESRD (a voucher used by one family member expires the vouchers of the other family members). I simulated two arms of healthy non-donors, both carrying non-donor baseline ESRD risk and a background all-cause mortality HR of 1.0. The sole difference between arms was waitlist priority upon ESRD onset: the voucher arm had a PLD mean wait of 148~days, derived from Wainright et~al.,~\cite{wainright_impact_2017} and the control arm had the standard mean wait of 1{,}765~days~\cite{punjala_determinants_2024}. Consistent with the sourcing rule used for the base-case preemptive-listing parameters, I assigned the voucher arm the informed/monitored preemptive-listing probability (9.4\%) and the control arm the general-population probability (5.8\%).~\cite{usrds_2025} All other transition probabilities (dialysis mortality, per-cycle waitlist listing, waitlist mortality and removal, post-transplant survival, graft failure) were identical to the base-case model. I ran the analysis at the base-case age of 40 with an age sweep and an ESRD-risk subgroup analysis using the Muzaale overall and Grams race-specific non-donor cumulative incidences.~\cite{muzaale_risk_2014,grams_kidney-failure_2016}

Because the voucher (and, by extension, the donor PLD-priority) benefit is diluted at the population level by the large fraction of the cohort who never develop ESRD, I ran a second model conditioned entirely on ESRD onset. All individuals entered the model already diagnosed with ESRD in order to isolate the life-expectancy benefit of priority waitlist access for the sub-population who actually reach the waitlist. Entry to dialysis versus preemptive listing, dialysis and waitlist mortality, waitlist removal, post-transplant survival, and graft failure used the same age-stratified, all-cause parameters as the base-case model; no separate Healthy state or background-mortality term was included, since dialysis- and waitlist-state mortality rates are all-cause rates already measured in ESRD populations. I compared two arms, differing only in waitlist priority (priority vs.\ standard median wait, as above). The base case used an ESRD-onset age of 60 with an age sweep.
