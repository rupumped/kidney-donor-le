\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Model overview}
I constructed a Markov state-transition model to compare lifetime outcomes for two hypothetical cohorts of otherwise identical individuals: one cohort that donates a kidney and one that does not. Both cohorts enter the model at the age of donation in full health, and the simulation advances in annual cycles until age~110. The analysis was conducted from the perspective of the individual donor rather than the health-care system; outcomes are reported as undiscounted remaining life-years, consistent with the convention in burden-of-disease analyses.~\cite{murray_gbd_2012} The base-case age at donation is 40 years; subgroup analyses span ages 25--55. I used a Monte Carlo simulation with $5 \times 10^6$ individuals per arm and cross-validated against a deterministic analytic cohort implementation. I used annual (one-year) cycles throughout.
% Life-years accumulated with an end-of-cycle convention; the resulting systematic underestimate of approximately 0.5~years applies equally to both arms and cancels in $\Delta$LE.
The Markov model structure is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:markov}.

\subsection{Health states}
The model contains seven mutually exclusive health states (Figure~\ref{fig:markov}): \textit{Healthy}, \textit{ESRD onset}, \textit{Dialysis}, \textit{Standard waitlist}, \textit{Priority waitlist}, \textit{Post-transplant}, and \textit{Dead} (absorbing). At ESRD onset, the cohort branches: a fraction is listed preemptively (bypassing dialysis entirely) and the remainder initiates hemodialysis. The donor arm routes ESRD survivors to the \textit{Priority waitlist}, reflecting the allocation priority afforded to prior living donors (PLD) under KAS250; the non-donor arm routes to the \textit{Standard waitlist}. Graft failure returns patients to the \textit{Dialysis} state, bypassing the ESRD-onset branch.

\subsection{ESRD risk after donation}
Fifteen-year cumulative ESRD incidence was taken from Muzaale et al.~\cite{muzaale_risk_2014}: 30.8 per 10,000 (95\%~CI 24.3--38.5) for donors and 3.9 per 10,000 (95\%~CI 0.8--8.9) for controls matched by age, sex, and health (8-fold relative risk). Race-specific donor rates were 74.7/10,000 (Black) and 22.7/10,000 (White).~\cite{muzaale_risk_2014} The White non-donor baseline was taken from Grams et al.~\cite{grams_kidney-failure_2016} (sex-averaged 0.05\%/15~yr), because zero ESRD events were recorded in White matched controls in Muzaale et al.

I modeled ESRD hazard with a Weibull distribution. I fit the shape parameter ($k = 2.54$) from the five cumulative-incidence-of-ESRD curves (median, interquartile range, and 1st/99th percentiles) that Massie et al.\ report at 5, 10, 15, and 20 years post-donation.~\cite{massie_quantifying_2017} The Weibull CDF linearizes under the complementary log-log transform, $\ln(-\ln(1-I(t))) = k\ln(t) - k\ln(\lambda)$, so ordinary least-squares regression of the transformed incidence on $\ln(t)$ recovers $k$ directly as the slope; applied to the median curve this gives $k=2.54$, reproducing all four published points to within 5\%. Fitting the same regression separately to each of the other four published curves gives $k$ ranging 2.15--2.54, confirming a single accelerating Weibull shape ($k>1$) is broadly consistent across the reported risk distribution; a constant-hazard ($k=1$) model is ruled out, as it would predict only $\approx$21/10,000 at 20 years from the observed 15-year rate of 16/10,000, versus the 34/10,000 actually reported. Once fit, $k$ was combined with a per-subgroup Weibull scale $\lambda$, calibrated via a competing-risk bisection algorithm to reproduce each subgroup's 15-year cumulative incidence while accounting for background-mortality attrition in the at-risk pool. I drew within-donor subgroup hazard ratios from Massie et al.: 2.96 (95\%~CI 2.25--3.89) for Black race, 1.88 (95\%~CI 1.50--2.35) for male sex, and 1.40 per decade (non-Black donors).~\cite{massie_quantifying_2017}

\subsection{Dialysis survival and waitlist listing}
\textcolor{red}{I stratified dialysis mortality by age. I took adjusted mortality among prevalent adult dialysis patients from the USRDS 2023 Annual Data Report (ADR) and converted rates to annual probabilities under a constant hazard: 6.8\%/year (ages 18--44), 11.4\% (45--64), 17.8\% (65--74), and 25.1\% ($\geq 75$).~\cite{usrds_2023} I used 2019 data rather than those from 2020--2021 because COVID-19 raised dialysis mortality by roughly 20\% in those years. I multiplied first-year mortality by 1.06, the ratio of year-1 to year-2 mortality in the 2019 incident hemodialysis cohort.~\cite{usrds_2023} As a check, these rates reproduce USRDS remaining life expectancy on dialysis within 15\% for every 5-year age group from 40--44 to 80--84.}

At ESRD onset, a fraction of patients is listed preemptively before dialysis initiation, avoiding dialysis mortality entirely. \textcolor{red}{For non-donors, I drew the preemptive listing probability from USRDS 2025 ADR: 5.8\% overall.~\cite{usrds_2025}}

\textcolor{red}{I derived the conditional annual probability of non-donors on dialysis joining the waitlist} from USRDS 2025 ADR.~\cite{usrds_2025} Approximately 25\% of 18--44 year-old ESRD patients are listed or transplanted within one year, \textcolor{red}{with $\approx$9.4\% of the ESRD cohort listed preemptively, so the conditional post-dialysis listing rate is $(25\%-9.4\%)/(100\%-9.4\%)\approx17\%$/year. Donor-arm values for both parameters are described in the next subsection.}

\subsection{\textcolor{red}{Outcomes after ESRD in prior donors}}
\label{sec:methods-donor-esrd}
\textcolor{red}{Registry inputs describe all ESRD patients and transplant candidates, not prior donors. Prior donors who develop ESRD are closely followed, are generally healthier, and are listed earlier than other ESRD patients.~\cite{muzaale2016outcomes} I therefore gave the donor arm donor-specific post-ESRD inputs from Muzaale et~al., who followed 99 US living donors who developed ESRD (median age at onset 50) and compared them with matched non-donors with ESRD.~\cite{muzaale2016outcomes}}

\textcolor{red}{Two inputs were taken directly from that study. First, 21 of 99 donors (21.2\%) avoided dialysis at onset (20 preemptive listings and one preemptive living-donor transplant), replacing the general preemptive-listing probability. Second, post-transplant mortality was multiplied by 0.7, the adjusted hazard ratio for prior donors versus matched non-donor recipients (95\%~CI 0.2--2.4).}

\textcolor{red}{Two further inputs were calibrated to the same study, with the cohort entering ESRD at age~50. For each candidate multiplier on donor dialysis mortality, I solved for the annual listing probability that reproduces the observed 43.4\% of donors listed within 12 months of ESRD. I kept the multiplier that minimized squared error against observed all-cause mortality 1, 3, 5, and 10~years after ESRD onset (9.1\%, 20.2\%, 25.5\%, and 32.4\%). The fit gave a dialysis-mortality multiplier of 0.78 and a listing probability of 0.31/year. The calibrated donor pathway predicts 7.6\%, 17.2\%, 23.8\%, and 37.2\% mortality at 1, 3, 5, and 10~years, respectively, all within the published 95\%~CIs.}

% As an independent check, the same pathway with general-population inputs reproduces 3- and 5-year mortality among Muzaale et~al.'s matched non-donors (25.9\% vs.\ 26.1\%; 37.4\% vs.\ 34.9\%), but under-predicts first-year mortality (10.6\% vs.\ 15.6\%) and over-predicts 10-year mortality (57.5\% vs.\ 43.0\%). This pattern is consistent with front-loaded deaths in a heterogeneous ESRD population, which a homogeneous cohort model does not capture. Differences in transplant access explain little of it: giving the general pathway the matched non-donors' 21.5-month median wait reduces 10-year mortality only from 57\% to 53\%.

\subsection{Transplant waiting time and waitlist outcomes}
Transplant probability while listed was modeled as a time-homogeneous exponential process with rate $r = 1/\mu$, where $\mu$ is the mean waiting time, the natural parameterization of the exponential distribution.~\cite{sonnenberg1993} \textcolor{red}{For the standard waitlist, I derived $\mu$ from SRTR 2023 ADR (2018--2020 listing cohort, 3-year outcomes) using the same competing exponential model applied to the removal rate: with total exit rate $\Sigma\lambda = -\ln(P_{\text{still}})/3 = 0.403$/year and transplant-specific rate $\lambda_T = (P_{\text{tx}}/P_{\text{exit}}) \times \Sigma\lambda = 0.255$/year (where $P_{\text{tx}} = 0.443$ accounts for both DDKT and LDKT), the implied mean is $\mu = 365.25/\lambda_T = 1{,}437$~days.~\cite{srtr_2023} Using this SRTR-calibrated value makes the transplant probability internally consistent with the competing removal rate, which was derived from the same figure. The one-way sensitivity range tests 24~months (730~days) and the pre-KAS250 historical mean of 1{,}857~days~\cite{punjala_determinants_2024} (Table~\ref{tab:owsa}); the Punjala~2024 post-KAS250 mean of 1{,}765~days~\cite{punjala_determinants_2024} provides a separate registry-based reference estimate.} For the PLD priority waitlist, I used the post-KAS median of 102.6~days,~\cite{wainright_impact_2017} converted to a mean via $\mu = \text{median}/\ln 2 \approx 148$~days. \textcolor{red}{I stratified waitlist mortality by age using 2019 pretransplant mortality among adult kidney candidates from SRTR 2023 ADR, chosen to match the USRDS dialysis year: 1.98 (ages 18--34), 2.95 (35--49), 4.97 (50--64), and 7.43 ($\geq 65$) deaths per 100 patient-years.~\cite{srtr_2023}} Competing removal (delisting for reasons other than transplant or death) was derived from the 3-year cumulative removal incidence of 19.06\% reported in SRTR 2023 ADR Figure~KI~22, back-calculated to a cause-specific annual rate of 11.4\%/year via competing-risk bisection accounting for simultaneous attrition from transplantation and death.~\cite{srtr_2023}

\subsection{Post-transplant survival}
I derived post-transplant annual mortality from age-stratified 5-year Kaplan--Meier estimates for DDKT recipients in the SRTR 2023 ADR (Figure~KI~70, 2016--2018 cohort): 0.9\%/year (ages 18--34), 1.8\%/year (35--49), 3.9\%/year (50--64), and 6.9\%/year ($\geq 65$).~\cite{srtr_2023} The base case uses DDKT survival because prior living donors who develop ESRD predominantly receive transplants via the deceased-donor priority pathway rather than living-donor transplantation, \textcolor{red}{consistent with Muzaale et~al., who found that prior donors predominantly receive deceased-donor kidneys (87\%) rather than living-donor transplants.~\cite{muzaale2016outcomes}} A one-way sensitivity analysis substitutes LDKT patient survival (SRTR 2023 ADR Figure~KI~76: 0.4\%, 0.8\%, 1.7\%, and 3.9\%/year by age band) to bound the effect of this assumption. Annual graft failure post-year~1 was likewise age-stratified, derived as $1-(S_5/S_1)^{1/4}$, where $S_n$ denotes the $n$-year graft survival probability, from age-specific 1- and 5-year DDKT graft survival in the SRTR 2023 ADR (Figure~KI~53, 2016--2018 cohort): 3.9\%/year (ages 18--34), 3.4\%/year (35--49), 4.9\%/year (50--64), and 7.8\%/year ($\geq 65$).~\cite{srtr_2023} Graft failure returned patients to the Dialysis state. \textcolor{red}{In the donor arm, post-transplant mortality was further scaled by the prior-donor hazard ratio.}

\subsection{\textcolor{red}{Age dependence and background floor for post-ESRD states}}
\textcolor{red}{All age-banded post-ESRD inputs (dialysis, waitlist, and post-transplant mortality, and graft failure) were assigned to the midpoint of each band and interpolated log-linearly between midpoints, held constant beyond the outermost midpoints. The lower-boundary bands (18--34 and 18--44) and the upper open-ended bands ($\geq$65 and $\geq$75) were assigned representative ages of 26, 31, 70, and 80 to avoid artifacts caused by step functions at band edges.} % Step functions at band edges produced artifacts. For example, post-transplant mortality jumping from 3.9\% to 6.9\% at exactly age~65 made the conditional value of priority access rise between ages 65 and 73.}

\textcolor{red}{In every post-ESRD state, annual mortality was also floored at the general-population life-table probability for the same age. Without this floor, state-specific rates fall below background mortality at advanced ages (e.g., 6.9\%/year post-transplant mortality at ages $\geq 65$ is exceeded by background mortality from age~83), which would make ESRD paradoxically protective late in life.}

\subsection{Background mortality and donor all-cause survival}
I took background mortality in the Healthy state from the 2021 US Life Tables,~\cite{cdc_2023} with sex-specific tables used in sex-stratified analyses. As discussed in the Introduction, the published literature on donor all-cause mortality does not consist of noisy estimates of one fixed hazard ratio (HR): short-to-medium-follow-up cohorts and the meta-analysis pooling them report no excess mortality,~\cite{okeeffe_mid-_2018} while the one cohort followed long enough to plausibly detect a late-emerging effect reports a significantly elevated HR,~\cite{mjoeen_long-term_2014} with survival curves separating only after approximately ten years.~\cite{mjoeen_reply,okeeffe_reply_2018} I therefore modeled the donor all-cause mortality HR as time-varying rather than fixed: flat at 1.0 (no excess risk) for the first 10 years after donation, then increasing linearly to 1.30 by 15 years post-donation, matching the Mjøen et~al.~\cite{mjoeen_long-term_2014} cohort's median follow-up, and held flat at 1.30 thereafter. The non-donor arm's background mortality HR is fixed at 1.0 throughout. Because this reflects a genuine question about model structure (does excess mortality risk exist at all, and if so, when does it emerge) rather than sampling variation around an agreed-upon value, it is not included in the probabilistic sensitivity analysis; instead, I report a dedicated structural sensitivity analysis over the plausible alternative HR assumptions in the literature.

\subsection{Subgroup analyses}
\textcolor{red}{I repeated the analyses at ages 25, 30, 35, 40, 45, 50, and 55~years at donation.} Donor ESRD rates were scaled by the Massie~2017 age HR (1.40/decade, non-Black donors)\textcolor{red}{; Massie et~al.\ found no significant age gradient among Black donors (HR 0.88/decade, 95\%~CI 0.72--1.09), so Black donor rates were held constant across age}.~\cite{massie_quantifying_2017} \textcolor{red}{Non-donor baselines were scaled by the age gradient of Grams et~al.'s 15-year projections for donor-like candidates at ages 20, 40, and 60, interpolated log-linearly by race and sex.~\cite{grams_kidney-failure_2016} These projections show Black non-donor risk rising steeply from age 20 to 40 but only $\approx$1.13-fold per decade from 40 to 60, against $\approx$1.45-fold for White candidates. The ``overall'' non-donor arm used in the age sweep keeps its Muzaale matched-control anchor at age~40 and uses the White gradient, since White donors make up most of the donor pool.}

I took race-specific donor ESRD rates (Black 74.7/10,000; White 22.7/10,000) and race-specific non-donor baselines (Black sex-averaged 0.195\%/15~yr; White sex-averaged 0.05\%/15~yr\textcolor{red}{, both at age~40}) from Muzaale et al.~\cite{muzaale_risk_2014} and Grams et al.,~\cite{grams_kidney-failure_2016} respectively. \textcolor{red}{Race-specific post-transplant survival was from SRTR 2023 Figure~KI~71. For waitlist mortality, the age-stratified rates were multiplied by the ratio of race-specific to overall 2019 pretransplant mortality (Figure~KI~26; Black 0.94, White 1.17), assuming race and age act multiplicatively.}~\cite{srtr_2023}

I derived sex-specific donor ESRD rates by applying the Massie~2017 male-sex HR (1.88)~\cite{massie_quantifying_2017} to the overall donor rate, weighted by the observed donor sex mix (63.5\% female; SRTR 2023 ADR Figure~KI~104).~\cite{srtr_2023} For non-donor baselines, I used the Grams~2016 White sex-specific rates (female 0.04\%/15~yr; male 0.06\%/15~yr\textcolor{red}{ at age~40, scaled by the sex-specific Grams age gradient})~\cite{grams_kidney-failure_2016} rather than applying the within-donor sex HR to the control arm, because Massie~2017 hazard ratios describe variation within the donor cohort and are not appropriate for scaling a different reference population. I applied this sourcing rule, Muzaale matched-control rates for overall analyses and Grams direct rates wherever sex or race is stratified\textcolor{red}{, with the Grams age gradient applied to every non-donor baseline}, consistently across all simulations.

\subsection{Probabilistic sensitivity analysis (PSA)}
\label{sec:methods-psa}
I conducted a PSA with 2,000 parameter draws. Sampled quantities and their distributions were:
\begin{itemize}
	\item ESRD 15-year cumulative risks: beta distributions parameterized from the Muzaale~2014 published 95\%~CIs;\cite{muzaale_risk_2014}
	\item Weibull shape $k$: log-normal, $\sigma = 0.069$, the sample standard deviation of $\ln k$ across the five independent cloglog-regression fits described above (Massie et al.\ does not report a CI for this parameter directly);
	\item Waitlist listing probability: beta, fractional SE~=~40\%, consistent with the 0.05--0.30 plausible range\textcolor{red}{; and}
	\item \textcolor{red}{Prior-donor post-ESRD inputs from Muzaale et~al.~(2016):~\cite{muzaale2016outcomes} preemptive fraction, beta with binomial SE (19\% of the mean); listing probability, beta with fractional SE~=~23\%, from the 95\%~CI of the proportion listed by 12 months; dialysis-mortality multiplier, log-normal with $\sigma = 0.234$, from the 95\%~CI of the overall mortality HR after ESRD (0.4--1.0); and post-transplant multiplier, log-normal with $\sigma = 0.634$, from its 95\%~CI (0.2--2.4).}
\end{itemize}
Registry-derived parameters (from SRTR or USRDS) have negligible sampling error relative to structural uncertainty and were fixed at base-case values; scenario variation in these parameters is captured by the one-way sensitivity analysis. The donor all-cause mortality HR is deliberately excluded from the PSA: as described above, the disagreement among published estimates reflects a structural question about the time-course of donor risk, not sampling error around a single value, and is instead addressed by a separate, dedicated structural sensitivity analysis. I report the mean $\Delta\text{LE}$, 95\%~credible interval, and the probability that donation is beneficial to life-expectancy.

\subsection{One-way sensitivity analysis}
I varied each parameter individually, holding the donor all-cause mortality HR at its base-case time-varying profile:
\begin{itemize}
	\item No priority access and changing the PLD wait time
	\item ESRD relative risk from the Mjøen et~al.\ point estimate on ESRD HR ($11\times$ controls)
	\item Post-transplant survival at LDKT quality
	\item Dialysis mortality~$\pm 50\%$
	\item \textcolor{red}{Prior-donor post-ESRD inputs replaced with the general ESRD population's (preemptive listing, listing rate, and dialysis and post-transplant mortality)}
	\item \textcolor{red}{Donor dialysis-mortality multiplier at the bounds of the Muzaale et~al.\ mortality HR (0.4 and 1.0)}
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Structural sensitivity to donor mortality hazard ratio}
\label{sec:methods-hr-structural}
Because the donor all-cause mortality HR is not sampled in the PSA or perturbed in the one-way sensitivity analysis above, I separately compared the base-case time-varying HR profile against the flat (time-invariant) HR implied by individual published estimates, taken at face value for the donor's entire remaining lifetime: 
% HR~$=0.60$ (the O'Keeffe et~al.\ pooled point estimate),~\cite{okeeffe_reply_2018} 
HR~$=1.0$ (no excess mortality), HR~$=1.30$ (the Mjøen et~al.\ point estimate),~\cite{mjoeen_long-term_2014} and HR~$=1.52$ (the upper bound of the Mjøen et~al.\ 95\%~CI). This analysis is intended to bound how much the result depends on which single-HR assumption is adopted, rather than to imply that any of these flat alternatives is itself a plausible model of donor risk.

I back-calculated the waitlist removal rate via a competing-risk bisection algorithm to reproduce the SRTR-observed 19.1\% removed at three years (11.4\%/year). The model over-predicted three-year waitlist mortality (10.5\% vs.\ 6.8\% observed, $\Delta = +54\%$), likely reflecting the application of a 2023 cross-sectional mortality rate (confirmed 5.0/100 patient-years from the raw SRTR data file) to a 2018--2020 listing cohort; this bias is symmetric across both arms and conservative (slightly overstates the cost of the waitlist period for both donors and non-donors equally). No independent cohort exists tracing the complete pathway from living donation through ESRD onset to transplantation; this check represents the available face-validity evidence for the model.

\subsection{Voucher benefit}
\label{subsec:methods-voucher}
A kidney-donation ``voucher'' is a mechanism by which a living donor designates up to five healthy, non-donor family members to receive PLD priority-waitlist access should any one of those family members ever develop ESRD (a voucher used by one family member expires the vouchers of the other family members). I simulated two arms of healthy non-donors, both carrying non-donor baseline ESRD risk and a background all-cause mortality HR of 1.0. \textcolor{red}{The arms differed in both waitlist priority and post-transplant survival type. The voucher arm used a priority mean wait of 148~days (the post-KAS250 median of 102.6~days from Wainright et~al.~\cite{wainright_impact_2017} converted via $\mu=\text{median}/\ln 2$; this estimate comes from a study of KAS250 deceased-donor access for prior living donors and is used here as a proxy for the shorter-than-standard wait enabled by an NKR living-donor chain), together with LDKT post-transplant patient survival (SRTR 2023 ADR Figure~KI~76: 0.4\%, 0.8\%, 1.7\%, and 3.9\%/year at ages 18--34, 35--49, 50--64, and $\geq 65$) and LDKT graft survival (Figure~KI~61), because NKR vouchers are designed to facilitate living-donor kidney exchange chains. The control arm used the SRTR-calibrated standard mean wait of 1{,}437~days~\cite{srtr_2023} and DDKT post-transplant survival, identical to the base-case non-donor arm. I assigned the voucher arm the USRDS preemptive-listing probability for ages 18--44 (9.4\%), as a proxy for an informed, monitored family member, and the control arm the general-population probability (5.8\%).~\cite{usrds_2025} All other transition probabilities (dialysis mortality, per-cycle waitlist listing, waitlist mortality and removal) were identical to the base-case non-donor arm; because voucher holders are not donors, neither arm used the prior-donor inputs.} I ran the analysis at the base-case age of 40 with an age sweep and an ESRD-risk subgroup analysis using the Muzaale overall and Grams race-specific non-donor cumulative incidences\textcolor{red}{, scaled to each entry age by the Grams age gradient}.~\cite{muzaale_risk_2014,grams_kidney-failure_2016}

Because the voucher (and, by extension, the donor PLD-priority) benefit is diluted at the population level by the large fraction of the cohort who never develop ESRD, I ran a second model conditioned entirely on ESRD onset. All individuals entered the model already diagnosed with ESRD in order to isolate the life-expectancy benefit of priority waitlist access for the sub-population who actually reach the waitlist. \textcolor{red}{Entry to dialysis versus preemptive listing (9.4\% priority arm, 5.8\% standard arm, as in the voucher analysis), dialysis and waitlist mortality, waitlist removal, and background mortality used the same age-dependent parameters as the base-case non-donor arm, including the life-table floor; I included no separate Healthy state.} \textcolor{red}{I compared two arms differing in both waitlist priority and post-transplant survival: the priority arm used the priority mean wait (148~days) and LDKT patient and graft survival (the same inputs as the voucher arm above), while the standard arm used the standard mean wait (1{,}437~days) and DDKT survival.} The base case used an ESRD-onset age of 60 with an age sweep.
