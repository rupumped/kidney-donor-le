\textbf{Background.} Living kidney donors face a small, well-documented increase in long-term risk of end-stage renal disease (ESRD), but this risk has not been expressed as life expectancy stratified by the demographic factors that modify it.

\textbf{Methods.} I constructed a Markov microsimulation model comparing lifetime outcomes for matched donor and non-donor cohorts, parameterized from US renal and transplant registries and published donor cohort studies, with probabilistic, one-way, and structural sensitivity analyses.

\textbf{Results.} Results depended chiefly on the assumed donor all-cause mortality hazard ratio (HR). Fixed published HRs placed the cost between under two months (HR~$=1.00$, consistent with shorter-follow-up cohorts) and over four years (upper confidence bound of the only long-follow-up cohort). A base case derived from the literature yielded a mean cost of 2.4~years, 84\% larger at age~25 than at age~55. Black donors' cost exceeded White donors' by 16\% at age~25 but not at age~55; men and women differed by about 2\% at age~40. Priority waitlist access was worth about 6~days to the average donor, and a National Kidney Registry voucher under two days to the average designated relative, but each was worth hundreds of days to those who developed ESRD.

\textbf{Limitations.} Results hinge on one cohort's HR estimate and its assumed onset time-course, and the model has face validity only, without external validation.

\textbf{Conclusions.} These findings support age-, race-, and sex-specific life-expectancy disclosure during living-donor consent, frame priority access as catastrophic insurance rather than a means of shifting average outcomes, and identify the time-course of donor mortality risk as the dominant research priority.
