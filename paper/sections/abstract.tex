\textbf{Background.} Living kidney donors face a small but well-documented elevation in long-term risk of end-stage renal disease (ESRD), yet this risk has not been translated into a decision-relevant life-expectancy metric stratified by the demographic factors known to modify it.

\textbf{Methods.} I constructed a Markov microsimulation model comparing lifetime outcomes for matched donor and non-donor cohorts, entering at the age of donation and simulated in annual cycles, parameterized from the United States Renal Data System (USRDS), the Scientific Registry of Transplant Recipients (SRTR), and published cohort studies of post-donation ESRD incidence and mortality.

\textbf{Results.} \textcolor{red}{The life-expectancy implications of the published risk estimates depend primarily on which published estimate of the donor all-cause mortality hazard ratio (HR) is assumed: the three shorter-follow-up cohorts in the largest meta-analysis are consistent with HR near~1.0, while the one cohort followed long enough to detect a late-emerging effect reports HR~$=1.30$ with excess mortality apparent only after approximately a decade. Holding the HR fixed at any single published value places the cost between under two months (HR~$=1.00$) and more than four years (the upper confidence bound of the long-follow-up estimate). A time-varying base case encoding this decade-long onset delay yields a mean cost of 2.4~years. This base-case cost was 84\% larger at age~25 than at age~55. Race and sex mattered less: Black donors' cost exceeded White donors' by 16\% at age~25, but the difference disappeared by age~55, and male and female donors differed by about 2\% \textcolor{red}{at the base-case age of~40}. Any other modeled parameter moves the result by a maximum of about five weeks.} \textcolor{red}{The life-expectancy benefit of prior-living-donor priority waitlist access was approximately 6~days unconditionally for the individual donor, and that of a National-Kidney-Registry-style voucher to designated non-donor family members was under two days on average; both were worth hundreds of days conditional on the designated beneficiaries actually developing ESRD.}

\textbf{Conclusions.} These findings support \textcolor{red}{age-, race-, and sex-specific} life-expectancy disclosure during living-donor informed consent, indicate that priority-access mechanisms function as catastrophic insurance rather than as interventions expected to shift average outcomes, and highlight resolving the time-course of donor all-cause mortality risk as a research priority, since it dominates every other source of uncertainty in this analysis.
