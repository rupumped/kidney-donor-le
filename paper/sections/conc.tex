\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
The mean life-expectancy cost to the individual donor ($\approx 37$~days) must be weighed against the benefit conferred on the recipient and, through the voucher mechanism, on designated family members who may one day develop ESRD. The ESRD-conditional model demonstrates that priority waitlist access alone is worth more than 200~days to a patient who will develop ESRD; the full benefit of receiving a functioning kidney rather than remaining on long-term haemodialysis is larger still. Viewed in this frame, donation represents a transfer of life expectancy from donor to recipient on highly favourable terms: the donor foregoes approximately five weeks of expected life in exchange for providing the recipient with the prospect of years of additional survival.

This aggregate figure, however, conceals substantial heterogeneity that bears directly on informed consent. The life-expectancy cost of donation is nearly three times larger for Black donors than for White donors, and more than three times larger at age~25 than at age~55; a young Black donor and an older White donor are, in effect, being asked to accept quantitatively different bargains under the same generic counseling framework. Because this disparity traces to a measurable, race-specific difference in post-donation ESRD incidence rather than to any modifiable feature of the transplant episode itself, donor evaluation and consent discussions arguably warrant demographic-specific life-expectancy estimates rather than a single population-average number.

The model also reframes what priority-access policy is actually protecting. KAS250 and the NKR voucher mechanism are designed around a rare event, an eventual ESRD diagnosis in a prior donor or designated family member, and this analysis shows that their unconditional, population-level life-expectancy value is correspondingly small (under one day for a voucher holder, under two days for donor PLD priority) simply because so few beneficiaries ever draw on it. This is not evidence that such policies are unimportant: conditional on the rare event they are designed for, priority access is worth hundreds of days, and the dilution reflects low incidence rather than low value per event. The appropriate way to communicate these mechanisms may therefore be closer to catastrophic insurance, whose expected value is small precisely because it is rarely invoked, than to an intervention expected to move average outcomes.

Several limitations temper these conclusions:
\begin{itemize}
	\item The results are dominated by a single structural uncertainty, the donor all-cause mortality hazard ratio, for which the largest available meta-analysis reports no elevation in mortality risk while the longest-follow-up single cohort reports a hazard ratio of 1.30; the qualitative sign of the donor's life-expectancy cost is robust to this uncertainty, but its magnitude is not, and the true value likely depends on donor-recipient relatedness and selection practices that differ across health systems and were not modelled here.
	\item The model reports only undiscounted life-years and does not incorporate quality-of-life weighting; because dialysis is associated with substantially reduced health utility, a QALY-adjusted analysis would likely widen, not narrow, the donor's disadvantage during any dialysis-dependent interval.
	\item Several parameters were necessarily borrowed across source populations under an explicit sourcing rule (e.g., within-donor hazard ratios from Massie et al.\ applied to the Muzaale matched-control baseline), and no single cohort traces the complete pathway from living donation through ESRD onset to transplantation, so the calibration checks reported here constitute face validity rather than external validation against a purpose-built dataset.
\end{itemize}
Future work should prioritize prospective donor cohorts with sufficient follow-up to estimate all-cause mortality directly.
