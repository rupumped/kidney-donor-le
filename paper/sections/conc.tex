\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
The mean life-expectancy cost to the individual donor ($\approx 911$~days, or 2.5~years) must be weighed against the benefit conferred on the recipient and, through the voucher mechanism, on designated family members who may one day develop ESRD. The ESRD-conditional model demonstrates that priority waitlist access alone is worth more than 108~days to a patient who develops ESRD at age~60; the full benefit of receiving a functioning kidney rather than remaining on long-term hemodialysis is larger still. Viewed in this frame, donation still represents a transfer of life expectancy from donor to recipient, but on markedly less favorable terms than a smaller base-case estimate would suggest: under the base-case time-varying mortality hazard ratio, the donor forgoes a substantially larger share of their own expected remaining life than the population-level priority-access benefit alone returns, in exchange for the recipient's prospect of years of additional survival.

This aggregate figure conceals heterogeneity, but less of it, and along a different axis, than a model driven mainly by ESRD-incidence differences would suggest. Age at donation is the demographic factor that matters most: the life-expectancy cost is roughly 91\% larger at age~25 relative to age~55, because a younger donor lives long enough after the roughly decade-long delay in donor mortality risk for a much larger share of their remaining life to fall under the elevated hazard ratio. Race and sex, which drove a three-fold and roughly 1.5-fold disparity respectively when post-donation ESRD incidence was the dominant cost driver, now differ by only about 7\% and about 3\% at the base-case donation age, because the ESRD-incidence differences that produce those disparities are a minor contributor once the much larger, demographically-neutral mortality-HR effect is accounted for. Age- and race-specific life-expectancy disclosure therefore both remain supported by this analysis, though on different grounds: a 25-year-old and a 55-year-old donor are, in effect, being asked to accept quantitatively different bargains under the same generic counseling framework, and a Black donor still faces a measurably larger cost than a White donor of the same age, most clearly so at younger donation ages. The racial gap is smaller than a model built around ESRD incidence alone would imply, and the possibility that donor mortality risk itself varies by race or by donor-recipient relatedness in ways this analysis could not evaluate (see Limitations) means the magnitude of the race-specific component should be treated as provisional rather than final, even where its direction is not.

The model also reframes what priority-access policy is actually protecting. KAS250 and the NKR voucher mechanism are designed around a rare event, an eventual ESRD diagnosis in a prior donor or designated family member, and this analysis shows that their unconditional, population-level life-expectancy value is correspondingly small (under one day for a voucher holder, under one day for donor PLD priority) simply because so few beneficiaries ever draw on it. This is not evidence that such policies are unimportant: conditional on the rare event they are designed for, priority access is worth 110 to over 500~days at the individual level (depending on age at ESRD onset), and the dilution reflects low incidence rather than low value per event. The appropriate way to communicate these mechanisms may therefore be closer to catastrophic insurance, whose expected value is small precisely because it is rarely invoked, than to an intervention expected to move average outcomes.

Several limitations temper these conclusions:
\begin{itemize}
	\item The results are dominated by a single structural uncertainty, the time-course of the donor all-cause mortality hazard ratio, rather than by sampling error in any individually estimated parameter. Short-to-medium-follow-up cohorts and the meta-analysis pooling them report no excess mortality, while the one cohort followed long enough to plausibly detect a late-emerging effect reports a hazard ratio of 1.30 that its own authors attribute to risk emerging only after roughly a decade. This analysis encodes that pattern as a piecewise-linear ramp (flat at 1.0 through year~10, rising to 1.30 by year~15, matching the Mjøen et~al.\ cohort's median follow-up), which is a minimal, directly literature-motivated functional form but an assumption nonetheless; the true time-course, and whether it is linear, could differ. Unlike most parameters in this model, this uncertainty is not confined to magnitude: taking the alternative literature point estimates at face value for the donor's entire life ranges from a multi-year life-expectancy \emph{benefit} (HR~$=0.60$) to a cost exceeding four years (the upper 95\%~CI of the long-follow-up estimate), so neither the size nor the sign of the donor's life-expectancy cost should be considered settled. The true value likely also depends on donor-recipient relatedness and selection practices that differ across health systems and were not modeled here.
	\item The model reports only undiscounted life-years and does not incorporate quality-of-life weighting; because dialysis is associated with substantially reduced health utility, a quality-adjusted life year (QALY)-adjusted analysis would likely widen, not narrow, the donor's disadvantage during any dialysis-dependent interval.
	\item Several parameters were necessarily borrowed across source populations under an explicit sourcing rule (e.g., within-donor hazard ratios from Massie et al.\ applied to the Muzaale matched-control baseline), and no single cohort traces the complete pathway from living donation through ESRD onset to transplantation, so the calibration checks reported here constitute face validity rather than external validation against a purpose-built dataset.
\end{itemize}
Future work should prioritize prospective donor cohorts with follow-up long enough, ideally beyond 15--20 years, to directly estimate whether and when donor all-cause mortality risk begins to rise, since this single question now determines whether donation carries a life-expectancy cost at all.
