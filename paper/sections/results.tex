\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Base-case life expectancy}
In the base-case analysis (age~40 at donation, overall population parameters, time-varying donor all-cause mortality HR as described in Methods), kidney donation was associated with a life-expectancy cost of $\Delta\text{LE} = -914.3$~days (2.50~years; donor 35.63~yr vs.\ non-donor 38.14~yr remaining life expectancy; Monte Carlo, $5 \times 10^6$ individuals per arm). The analytic cohort confirmed this estimate ($\Delta\text{LE} = -911.8$~days). Across all base-case and subgroup scenarios, donation was associated with a net life-expectancy cost to the donor under this base-case HR assumption; no scenario identified donation as neutral or beneficial to the individual, with the exception of an alternative structural HR assumption examined below (``Structural sensitivity: donor mortality hazard ratio'').

\subsection{Racial disparity}
Race was a comparatively minor modifier of life-expectancy cost once the donor all-cause mortality HR, which affects all donors equally regardless of race, dominates the base-case result (Figure~\ref{fig:age_race_sex_matrix}). At age~40, Black donors experienced a cost of 950.7~days, about 7\% larger than the 892.5~days estimated for White donors. This gap was wider in relative terms at younger donation ages and closed entirely, with a slight reversal, at older ages: a Black donor at age~25 loses 1{,}404.0~days of life expectancy compared with 1{,}094.8~days for a White donor of the same age (a 28\% difference), while at age~55 the two estimates are essentially identical and marginally reversed (586.7 vs.\ 588.8~days). This residual disparity traces to post-donation ESRD incidence: Black donors have a 15-year cumulative incidence of 74.7 per~10,000 versus 22.7 per~10,000 in White donors,~\cite{muzaale_risk_2014} a $3.3\times$ difference further compounded by a higher background ESRD rate in age- and health-matched Black non-donors,~\cite{grams_kidney-failure_2016} but this ESRD-driven gap is now a minor contributor relative to the much larger, race-neutral cost imposed by the time-varying mortality HR.

\subsection{Age at donation}
\label{sec:results-age}
The life-expectancy cost decreased monotonically with older age at donation, from 1{,}131.6~days at age~25 to 591.2~days at age~55, roughly 91\% larger at the youngest age relative to the oldest (Figure~\ref{fig:age_race_sex_matrix}). Unlike the racial and sex disparities, this age gradient is driven primarily by the time-varying mortality HR rather than by ESRD risk: a younger donor lives long enough after the ten-year onset window for a much larger share of their remaining lifetime to fall under the elevated (up to 1.30) hazard ratio, whereas an older donor's remaining life expectancy is increasingly concentrated in the years before the HR has fully risen.

\subsection{Sex}
Male and female donors faced broadly similar life-expectancy costs at age~40 (915.9~days for male donors vs.\ 888.3~days for female donors, a difference of about 3\%), even though male donors carry a within-donor ESRD hazard ratio of 1.88 relative to female donors.~\cite{massie_quantifying_2017} As with race, the sex-specific ESRD risk difference that dominated this comparison under a flat mortality HR is now a minor contributor next to the much larger, sex-neutral cost imposed by the time-varying mortality HR: a Black male donor at age~40 loses 971.3~days, about 6\% more than the 915.9~days for an overall male donor and about 10\% more than the 880.7~days for a White female donor of the same age (Figure~\ref{fig:age_race_sex_matrix}). At age~25, Black male donors lose 1{,}506.8~days versus 1{,}048.4~days for White female donors, a 44\% difference that is larger than at age~40 for the same reason the racial disparity is larger at younger ages (see ``Age at donation,'' above).

\subsection{Probabilistic sensitivity analysis}
The PSA of 2,000~parameter draws (Figure~\ref{fig:psa}), which holds the donor all-cause mortality HR fixed at its base-case time-varying profile (Methods), produced a mean $\Delta\text{LE}$ of $-910.8$~days (95\%~credible interval $-936.1$ to $-889.3$~days; P[donation beneficial]~$= 0\%$). With the HR held fixed, the residual parameter uncertainty captured by the PSA (ESRD incidence, Weibull shape, waitlist listing probability) is narrow and consistently negative, in sharp contrast to the much larger uncertainty introduced by the choice of HR assumption itself (see ``Structural sensitivity: donor mortality hazard ratio,'' below).

\subsection{One-way sensitivity analysis}
\label{sec:owsa}
With the donor all-cause mortality HR held fixed at its base-case time-varying profile rather than perturbed (Methods), no single parameter in the one-way sensitivity analysis produced a swing exceeding 42~days (Table~\ref{tab:owsa}, Figure~\ref{fig:tornado}). The largest remaining drivers were the donor ESRD relative risk (ranging from $-867.8$~days, $+41.5$~days vs.\ base, at $2\times$ controls to $-932.3$~days, $-23.0$~days vs.\ base, at the Mjøen upper-bound $11\times$) and dialysis mortality ($\pm 50\%$, ranging from $-873.4$~days, $+35.9$~days vs.\ base, to $-927.3$~days, $-18.0$~days vs.\ base). All other parameters, including PLD and standard waitlist wait times and post-transplant graft quality ($+19.5$~days at LDKT quality), shifted $\Delta\text{LE}$ by under 20~days. This is a direct consequence of removing the mortality HR from the sensitivity analysis: every scenario in Table~\ref{tab:owsa} shares the same base-case HR profile, so none of them can reproduce the order-of-magnitude swings that the HR assumption itself produces (see below).

\subsection{Structural sensitivity: donor mortality hazard ratio}
\label{sec:results-hr-structural}
Because the donor all-cause mortality HR is excluded from both the PSA and the one-way sensitivity analysis above, I separately compared the base-case time-varying HR profile against the flat HR implied by each individual published estimate, applied for the donor's entire remaining lifetime (Figure~\ref{fig:hr_scenarios}). Taking the O'Keeffe et~al.\ pooled point estimate (HR~$=0.60$) at face value and applying it for life, rather than treating it as evidence of no excess risk within the follow-up window it covers, reverses the sign of the result entirely: donation is associated with a life-expectancy \emph{benefit} of 1{,}855.8~days. The textbook ``no excess mortality'' assumption (HR~$=1.0$ flat) yields a cost of only 68.3~days, an order of magnitude smaller than the time-varying base case. The base-case time-varying profile (1.0 ramping to 1.30 over years 10--15) yields $-909.3$~days. Applying the Mjøen et~al.\ point estimate (HR~$=1.30$) from the moment of donation, rather than only after the ten-year lag their own data support, yields $-1{,}084.5$~days. The upper bound of the Mjøen et~al.\ 95\%~CI (HR~$=1.52$), applied flat, yields $-1{,}694.0$~days. This 3{,}550-day range, from a multi-year benefit to a multi-year cost, dwarfs every other source of uncertainty examined in this analysis and is the dominant open question for any model of donor life expectancy.

\subsection{Donor priority benefit}
Although KAS250 affords prior living donors (PLD) priority access to the deceased-donor waitlist,~\cite{wainright_impact_2017} the resultant life-expectancy benefit to the donor is small at the population level. Removing PLD priority access entirely, setting the donor waitlist to the standard 1{,}765-day mean wait, worsened $\Delta\text{LE}$ by 0.2~days relative to base case. Reducing the priority wait to 50~days (median, equivalent to a mean of $\approx$72~days) had negligible additional effect. This insensitivity to the exact priority wait time arises because fewer than 0.3\% of donors develop ESRD within 15~years and fewer still ever reach the transplant waitlist; the priority pathway is simply too rarely invoked to move a population-level outcome meaningfully.

\subsection{Voucher benefit}
The life-expectancy benefit of a kidney-donation voucher to a healthy, non-donor family member peaked at $+1.2$~days for a recipient designated at age~25, falling to $+0.3$~days at age~40 and becoming negligible by age~60. These small figures reflect the same dilution mechanism: conditional on developing ESRD and reaching the priority waitlist, priority access adds a clinically substantial $+539$~days of life expectancy for a patient whose ESRD onset occurs at age~40, and $+109$~days for onset at age~60 (Figure~\ref{fig:esrd_conditional}). However, since only approximately 0.04\% of non-donors develop ESRD within 15~years, this large conditional benefit compresses to well under one day at the population level. Voucher benefit was modestly higher for Black non-donor family members ($+1.5$~days at age~40), owing to their approximately four-fold higher baseline ESRD incidence.~\cite{grams_kidney-failure_2016}
