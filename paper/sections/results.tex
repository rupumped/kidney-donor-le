\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Base-case life expectancy}
\textcolor{red}{In the base-case analysis, kidney donation was associated with a life-expectancy cost of $\Delta\text{LE} = -891.3$~days.} \textcolor{red}{Across all base-case and subgroup scenarios, donation was associated with a net life-expectancy cost to the donor under this base-case HR assumption.}

\subsection{Racial disparity}
Race was a comparatively minor modifier of life-expectancy cost once the donor all-cause mortality HR, which affects all donors equally regardless of race, dominates the base-case result (Figure~\ref{fig:age_race_sex_matrix}). \textcolor{red}{At age~40, Black donors experienced a cost of 896.5~days, about 2\% larger than the 877.3~days estimated for White donors. This gap was wider at younger donation ages and closed at older ages: a Black donor at age~25 loses 1{,}246.9~days of life expectancy compared with 1{,}069.7~days for a White donor of the same age (a 16\% difference), while at age~55 the two estimates are essentially equal (583.5 vs.\ 587.0~days).} This disparity \textcolor{red}{at younger ages} traces to post-donation ESRD incidence: Black donors have a 15-year cumulative incidence of 74.7 per~10,000 versus 22.7 per~10,000 in White donors,~\cite{muzaale_risk_2014} a $3.3\times$ difference further compounded by a higher background ESRD rate in age- and health-matched Black non-donors.~\cite{grams_kidney-failure_2016} \textcolor{red}{The closing of the gap at older donation ages reflects a significant age$\times$race interaction reported by Massie et~al.: among non-Black donors, post-donation ESRD risk rises with age at donation (HR~$=1.40$/decade, 95\%~CI 1.23--1.59), but among Black donors, no age gradient was detected (HR~$=0.88$/decade, 95\%~CI 0.72--1.09).~\cite{massie_quantifying_2017} Non-donor projections show a weaker version of the same pattern, with Black risk flattening after age~40.~\cite{grams_kidney-failure_2016} As a result, Black donors' excess 15-year ESRD incidence over matched non-donors narrows from 66 to 51 per~10,000 between ages 25 and 55, while White donors' widens from 12 to 29 per~10,000.} Massie et~al.\ note this flat age effect in Black donors may partly reflect a selection effect at donor evaluation, wherein older Black candidates predisposed to CKD are more likely to be screened out before donation, rather than an absence of age-related risk in the general population.~\cite{massie_quantifying_2017}

\subsection{Age at donation}
\label{sec:results-age}
\textcolor{red}{The life-expectancy cost decreased monotonically with older age at donation, from 1{,}092.6~days at age~25 to 592.4~days at age~55, roughly 84\% larger at the youngest age relative to the oldest (Figure~\ref{fig:age_race_sex_matrix}).} Unlike the racial and sex disparities, this age gradient is driven primarily by the time-varying mortality HR rather than by ESRD risk: a younger donor lives long enough after the ten-year onset window for a much larger share of their remaining lifetime to fall under the elevated (up to 1.30) hazard ratio, whereas an older donor's remaining life expectancy is increasingly concentrated in the years before the HR has fully risen.

\subsection{Sex}
\textcolor{red}{Male and female donors faced broadly similar life-expectancy costs at age~40 (891.6~days for male donors vs.\ 872.5~days for female donors, a difference of about 2\%), even though male donors carry a within-donor ESRD hazard ratio of 1.88 relative to female donors.}~\cite{massie_quantifying_2017} \textcolor{red}{The sex-specific ESRD risk difference is a minor contributor next to the much larger cost imposed by the time-varying mortality HR, which is applied identically to male and female donors: a Black male donor at age~40 loses 902.2~days, about 1\% more than the 891.6~days for an overall male donor and about 5\% more than the 862.2~days for a White female donor of the same age (Figure~\ref{fig:age_race_sex_matrix}). At age~25, Black male donors lose 1{,}291.3~days versus 1{,}026.4~days for White female donors, a 26\% difference that is larger than at age~40 for the same reason the racial disparity is larger at younger ages.}

\subsection{Probabilistic sensitivity analysis}
\textcolor{red}{The PSA of 2,000~parameter draws (Figure~\ref{fig:psa}), which holds the donor all-cause mortality HR fixed at its base-case time-varying profile, produced a mean $\Delta\text{LE}$ of $-896.7$~days (95\%~credible interval $-941.4$ to $-863.0$~days; P[donation beneficial]~$= 0\%$). With the HR held fixed, the residual parameter uncertainty captured by the PSA (ESRD incidence, Weibull shape, waitlist listing probability, and prior-donor post-ESRD inputs) is narrow and consistently negative.}

\subsection{One-way sensitivity analysis}
\label{sec:owsa}
\textcolor{red}{With the donor all-cause mortality HR held fixed at its base-case time-varying profile rather than perturbed, no single parameter in the one-way sensitivity analysis produced a swing exceeding 35~days (Table~\ref{tab:owsa}, Figure~\ref{fig:tornado}). The largest was replacing the prior-donor post-ESRD inputs with those of the general ESRD population ($-32.7$~days), followed by the donor ESRD relative risk ($+33.0$~days at $2\times$; $+21.7$~days at $4\times$), the donor dialysis-mortality multiplier ($+24.5$~days at 0.4), and dialysis mortality~$\pm 50\%$ ($-16.1$ to $+20.1$~days). All remaining parameters, including PLD and standard waitlist wait times and post-transplant graft quality, shifted $\Delta\text{LE}$ by under 10~days.}

% \textcolor{red}{The donor-specific inputs matter mainly for the share of the cost that runs through ESRD. With no excess all-cause mortality (HR~$=1.0$ flat), the cost of donation is 54.7~days using prior-donor inputs and 92.2~days using general ESRD inputs; general-population registry inputs would overstate the ESRD-attributable cost by about 70\%.}

\subsection{Structural sensitivity to donor mortality hazard ratio}
\label{sec:results-hr-structural}
I separately compared the base-case time-varying HR profile against the flat HR implied by individual published estimates, applied for the donor's entire remaining lifetime (Figure~\ref{fig:hr_scenarios}).
% Taking the O'Keeffe et~al.\ pooled point estimate (HR~$=0.60$)\cite{okeeffe_reply_2018} at face value and applying it for life, rather than treating it as evidence of no excess risk within the follow-up window it covers, reverses the result: donation is associated with a life-expectancy benefit of 1{,}855.8~days.
\textcolor{red}{The textbook ``no excess mortality'' assumption (HR~$=1.0$ flat) yields a cost of only 55.0~days, an order of magnitude smaller than the time-varying base case. The base-case time-varying profile (1.0 ramping to 1.30 over years 10--15) yields $-891.3$~days. Applying the Mjøen et~al.\ point estimate (HR~$=1.30$) from the moment of donation, rather than only after the ten-year lag their own data support, yields $-1{,}066.5$~days. The upper bound of the Mjøen et~al.\ 95\%~CI (HR~$=1.52$), applied flat, yields $-1{,}674.2$~days. This 1{,}619-day range, from a cost of under two months to a cost exceeding four years,} dwarfs every other source of uncertainty examined in this analysis and is the dominant open question for any model of donor life expectancy.

\subsection{Donor priority benefit}
Although KAS250 affords prior living donors (PLD) priority access to the deceased-donor waitlist,~\cite{wainright_impact_2017} the resultant life-expectancy benefit to the donor is small at the population level. \textcolor{red}{Removing PLD priority access entirely, setting the donor waitlist to the standard 1{,}437-day mean wait, worsened $\Delta\text{LE}$ by 6.2~days relative to base case.} Reducing the priority wait to 50~days (median, equivalent to a mean of $\approx$72~days) had negligible additional effect. This insensitivity to the exact priority wait time arises because \textcolor{red}{approximately 0.31\%} of donors develop ESRD within 15~years and fewer still ever reach the transplant waitlist; the priority pathway is simply too rarely invoked to move a population-level outcome meaningfully.

\subsection{Voucher benefit}
\textcolor{red}{The life-expectancy benefit of a kidney-donation voucher to a healthy, non-donor family member was small: $+1.92$~days at age~25, $+1.47$~days at age~40, and $+0.34$~days at age~60. This reflects the same dilution mechanism: conditional on developing ESRD and reaching the priority waitlist, priority access via an NKR living-donor chain adds a clinically substantial $+1{,}488$~days of life expectancy for a patient whose ESRD onset occurs at age~40, and $+608$~days for onset at age~60, declining steadily with onset age (Figure~\ref{fig:esrd_conditional}).} However, since only approximately 0.04\% of non-donors develop ESRD within 15~years, this large conditional benefit compresses to under two days at the population level. \textcolor{red}{Voucher benefit was higher for Black non-donor family members ($+7.30$~days at age~40)}, owing to their approximately four-fold higher baseline ESRD incidence.~\cite{grams_kidney-failure_2016}
